Turn-Taking Band

A Wearable Device Supporting Turn-Takings in Hybrid Settings

Overview

Hybrid work brings together people in different physical locations, but differences in communication cues and interaction patterns can make it difficult for remote participants to take part in discussions. This project explores turn-taking challenges in hybrid collaboration, focusing on how participants coordinate conversations and maintain mutual engagement across locations.

Through an exploratory exercise including a hybrid collaborative game, ethnographic methods were used to examine participant interactions. The study involved eight participants across the University of Copenhagen and the University of Maryland, with data collected through observations, video recordings, transcripts, interviews, and surveys.

The findings revealed three challenges in hybrid turn-taking: difficulties in finding opportunities to join discussions, identifying and resolving audio issues, and maintaining focus across locations. Based on the findings, a paper prototype of Turn-Taking Band, a wearable interaction concept, was proposed, using visual and vibration feedback to indicate speaking turns. The project also raises questions about accessibility, adoption, and the potential constraints of introducing structured turn-taking into hybrid meetings.

Why this matters

  • Hybrid collaboration brings together people in different physical locations, but differences in interaction settings can make communication less balanced.
  • Remote participants may struggle to find opportunities to join discussions, as they have less access to non-verbal cues and informal exchanges among co-located participants.
  • Audio difficulties and divided attention can further disrupt turn-taking, making it harder for participants across locations to stay engaged in collaborative activities.

Research question: What turn-taking challenges emerge in a hybrid setting, and how might technology support them?

Study & Data Collection

We conducted an exploratory exercise across the University of Copenhagen and the University of Maryland. Our team was responsible for the Werewolf game, involving five participants at the University of Copenhagen (including the game master) and three participants at the University of Maryland. The two groups interacted through Zoom, with a shared screen and monitoring setup connecting the locations (Figure 1).

We used ethnographic methods to examine participants' interactions. Data included video recordings, transcripts, photographs, observation notes, semi-structured interviews, and post-exercise survey responses. The analysis focused on changes in conversation partners, periods of silence, and differences in participation between the two locations.

Findings

Difficulties in joining discussions

Remote participants sometimes remained silent while co-located participants continued discussing. They could be unsure when to contribute and concerned that speaking at the same time as others would cause interruptions or overlapping audio. Verbal invitations alone did not always resolve this difficulty.

Difficulties in identifying and resolving audio issues

Audio problems were not always immediately noticeable to the participants experiencing them. In some cases, microphone issues were only identified after participants checked the chat or received feedback from the other location, interrupting the flow of communication.

Difficulties in maintaining focus

Differences in schedules, fatigue, and attention affected participants' readiness to collaborate. The report also notes that quiet periods could reflect either game-related decision-making or reduced engagement, making silence difficult to interpret without considering the activity context.

Design: Turn-Taking Band

Based on the findings, we used ideation techniques such as 'worst possible idea' and 'round robin' to explore possible solutions. We then developed a paper prototype with two connected components (Figure 2).

  • Wearable band: Uses LED lights and vibration patterns to communicate speaking status. Red indicates that it is not the participant's turn, yellow shows their position in the queue, and green signals that they can speak.
  • Speaker highlighting system: Uses on-screen visual cues to identify the current speaker and show who is waiting in the queue.

When someone speaks, participants at the other location receive two short vibrations as a reminder to pay attention. The on-screen highlighting system further supports awareness of who is speaking and who is waiting (Figure 3).

Discussion & Limitations

The design explores how wearable interaction and visual feedback might make speaking opportunities more visible. However, introducing a structured turn-taking protocol also creates trade-offs:

  • Accessibility and adoption: The design assumes access to a wearable device with light and vibration functions, and would require integration with common meeting platforms.
  • Interaction flexibility: A fixed speaking queue could limit spontaneous exchanges and make informal conversations or collaborative activities feel less natural.
  • Potential distraction: Feedback that is too frequent or poorly timed could interrupt rather than support collaboration.
  • Evaluation limitations: The study was exploratory, and camera placement made some non-verbal cues, such as facial expressions and gaze, difficult to capture. The proposed paper prototype was not empirically evaluated in real-world meetings.

This project highlights a tension between making participation more structured and preserving the spontaneity of conversation. Designing for hybrid collaboration therefore requires considering not only how to distribute speaking opportunities, but also how to maintain the flexibility of social interaction.
